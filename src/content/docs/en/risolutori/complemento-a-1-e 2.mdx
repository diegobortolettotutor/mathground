Given a binary number of $4$, $8$, $12$ or $16$ bits, this solver computes its
**ones' complement** and **two's complement**, shows how the three numbers are read as
signed values, and checks the result by adding the starting number to its complements.

## What they are for

A computer stores numbers as bit sequences of fixed length, for example $8$ or $16$.
With $n$ bits the natural numbers from $0$ to $2^{n}-1$ can be written; to represent
**negative** numbers as well, a rule is needed that states which sequences stand for a
negative number. Ones' complement and two's complement are two such rules: in both the
leftmost bit gives the sign ($0$ positive, $1$ negative), and the opposite of a number
is obtained with a simple operation on its bits.

### Ones' complement

- It is obtained by **inverting all the bits**: every $0$ becomes $1$ and every $1$
  becomes $0$.
- With $n$ bits it represents the numbers from $-(2^{n-1}-1)$ to $2^{n-1}-1$; with $8$
  bits, from $-127$ to $127$.
- Zero has **two patterns**: all bits $0$ (positive zero) and all bits $1$ (negative
  zero).
- In an addition, the carry leaving the leftmost bit is not discarded: it is added back
  to the result.
- Where it is used today: in the **checksum** of IPv4, UDP and TCP headers, the field
  the receiver of a packet uses to check that the header was not damaged in
  transmission. The checksum is the ones' complement of the ones' complement sum of the
  header's $16$-bit words. Ones' complement is also the first step in computing the
  two's complement.

### Two's complement

- It is obtained by **adding $1$ to the ones' complement**. Alternatively: starting from
  the right, the bits are copied up to and including the first $1$, and all the others
  are inverted.
- With $n$ bits it represents the numbers from $-2^{n-1}$ to $2^{n-1}-1$; with $8$ bits,
  from $-128$ to $127$.
- Zero has **a single pattern**.
- The subtraction $A - B$ is done as the addition $A + C_2(B)$, with the same circuit
  that performs additions; the carry leaving the leftmost bit is discarded. This is why
  two's complement is how all modern processors represent signed integers.

Ranges representable with the bit counts of the solver:

| Number of bits | Ones' complement | Two's complement |
| :-: | :-: | :-: |
| $4$ | from $-7$ to $7$ | from $-8$ to $7$ |
| $8$ | from $-127$ to $127$ | from $-128$ to $127$ |
| $12$ | from $-2047$ to $2047$ | from $-2048$ to $2047$ |
| $16$ | from $-32767$ to $32767$ | from $-32768$ to $32767$ |

For addition and subtraction of signed numbers, with the overflow check, see the page
[Addition and subtraction in two's and ones' complement](/en/risolutori/somma-e-sottrazione-in-complemento/).

## Solver

Choose the number of bits and enter the bits in the boxes, which all start at $0$. The
quick result reports the three numbers, in binary and in decimal; the step-by-step
solution shows the bit inversion, the addition with carries, the check with the quick
rule, the signed reading, and the sum of the number with its complements.
