Bubble Sort sorts a list by comparing neighbouring elements only. The list is scanned from left to
right and, whenever two neighbours are in the wrong order, they are swapped. At the end of each
pass the largest value among those not yet in place has reached the end, like a bubble rising to
the surface: hence the name.

The simulator accepts from 2 to 10 whole numbers between 0 and 99, separated by commas or spaces,
or it can generate random ones. When the simulator has the focus, the arrow keys move one step
forwards or backwards.

## The procedure

Given a list $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}$ of $n$ elements:

1. all pairs of neighbours $a_j, a_{j+1}$ in the part still to sort are compared from left to
   right; if $a_j > a_{j+1}$, the two elements are swapped;
2. at the end of the pass the last element of the part still to sort is in its final place, and
   the part still to sort becomes one element shorter;
3. if the pass made no swaps, the list is already sorted and the algorithm stops; otherwise it
   goes back to step 1.

At most $n - 1$ passes are needed: once the last $n - 1$ elements are in place, the first one is
in place too.

## Why the maximum reaches the end

During a pass, the largest value met so far is always greater than its right-hand neighbour, so it
is swapped and keeps moving to the right. Once reached, the maximum of the part still to sort does
not stop until the last position. In the initial example this can be seen in the first pass: 73 is
carried to the right until it meets 92, which from that point goes all the way to the end.

## How many comparisons and how many swaps

Pass $p$ compares $n - p$ pairs. In the worst case all $n - 1$ passes are needed, and the
comparisons are

$$
(n-1) + (n-2) + \dots + 1 = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}
$$

that is 21 with 7 elements. In the best case, with the list already sorted, the first pass makes
no swaps and $n - 1$ comparisons are enough.

The number of swaps has a precise meaning. An **inversion** is a pair of elements, not
necessarily neighbours, in which the larger one comes before the smaller one. Swapping two
neighbours changes only the order between those two, so it removes exactly one inversion. The
swaps are therefore as many as the inversions of the starting list: from $0$ for a list already
sorted up to $n(n-1)/2$ for a list in decreasing order.

| Example | Inversions | Passes | Comparisons | Swaps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| simple: 41, 73, 17, 56, 8, 92, 29 | 11 | 5 | 20 | 11 |
| advanced: 96, 74, 81, 53, 38, 45, 19 | 19 | 6 | 21 | 19 |

In the simple example the fifth pass makes no swaps and the algorithm stops one pass before the
maximum. In any case the number of comparisons grows like $n^2$: doubling the length of the list
roughly quadruples the work in the worst case.

:::tip[Worth noticing]
Large values rise quickly, small values sink slowly: a small element moves to the left by a single
position per pass. In the advanced example 19 starts in the last cell and reaches the first one
only in the sixth pass, which is why every possible pass is needed.
:::

## Properties

- The algorithm works **in place**: it modifies the list itself and uses only one auxiliary
  variable for the swap.
- The algorithm is **stable**: two equal elements keep their original relative order. A swap
  happens only if $a_j > a_{j+1}$, so two equal values are never swapped. Stability matters when
  sorting data with several fields: a list of students already in alphabetical order, then sorted
  by mark with a stable algorithm, stays in alphabetical order among students with the same mark.

## A bit of history

The earliest known description of the algorithm is in a 1956 paper by Edward H. Friend in the
*Journal of the ACM*, where it appears as *sorting by exchange*. The name *bubble sort* first
appears in print in 1962, in a book by Kenneth E. Iverson. Donald Knuth, in *The Art of Computer
Programming*, concluded that the algorithm has little to recommend it apart from its catchy name;
it is still one of the most common examples in teaching, because it is simple to describe and to
prove correct.

Source: [Owen Astrachan, *Bubble Sort: An Archaeological Algorithmic Analysis*, SIGCSE 2003](https://users.cs.duke.edu/~ola/bubble/bubble.html).
