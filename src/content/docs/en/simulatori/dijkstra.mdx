On a directed graph the numbers on the edges are travel times in minutes. The problem is to
find the path from S to T that takes the least time. The algorithm does not know the shape
of the network in advance: at the start it only sees S and its outgoing edges, and it
discovers the other nodes as it reaches them. There is no need, however, to try every
possible path: each edge is examined exactly once, and at the end the shortest path is
certain.

## What each node stores

A graph drawn with nodes and edges alone is not enough to follow the algorithm. Each node
carries a **label** with three fields, like the fields of an object:

- **cost from S**: the lowest cost known so far to reach the node starting from S. At the
  start it is $0$ for S and $\infty$ for every other node;
- **via**: the previous node along the path that achieves that cost, i.e. the
  **predecessor**;
- **state**: not reached yet, tentative (the node is in the queue) or final.

Cost and predecessor are overwritten every time a cheaper path is found. Once the node
becomes final, the two values no longer change.

## The priority queue

The **priority queue** is a single list holding the nodes that have been discovered but are
not final yet, sorted by increasing cost from S. Each node appears only once. The value used
for sorting is the **total** cost from S, not the weight of a single edge: a node reached
from A through an edge of weight 4 enters the queue with cost $4 + 4 = 8$, because A itself
costs 4.

At every step the first element is removed, i.e. the node with the lowest cost from S. No
path is followed to its end: after removing A and discovering B at cost 8, the next node is
D, which costs 6. The other neighbours of S are not "pending branches" to be exhausted in a
fixed order, but plain elements of the list, which leave when their cost is the lowest.

## The procedure

1. S is inserted into the queue with cost from S equal to $0$.
2. While the queue is not empty, the node $x$ with the lowest cost from S is removed and
   marked as final.
3. For every edge $x \to v$ of weight $w$, the value $c = d(x) + w$ is computed, where
   $d(x)$ is the cost from S of $x$:
    - if $v$ is already final, nothing is done;
    - if $v$ is not in the queue yet, it is inserted with cost $c$ and predecessor $x$;
    - if $v$ is in the queue with a cost greater than $c$, cost and predecessor are updated
      and $v$ is moved to its new position in the queue;
    - otherwise nothing is done.
4. The procedure goes back to step 2.

Step 3 is called edge **relaxation** and boils down to a single rule:

$$
d(v) \leftarrow \min\big(d(v),\; d(x) + w(x, v)\big)
$$

In the simulator it can be seen at the step where the edge B→E is examined: the cost of E
drops from 12 to 9 and the entry of E moves up the queue, overtaking G.

## Why a final cost never changes

Suppose node $x$ leaves the queue with cost from S equal to $d(x)$, and consider any other
path $P$ from S to $x$. Since S is final and $x$ is not yet, $P$ must leave the set of final
nodes at some point: let $y$ be the first non-final node along $P$.

- The node preceding $y$ along $P$ is final, so its edge to $y$ has already been relaxed:
  $d(y)$ does not exceed the cost of the part of $P$ from S to $y$.
- $x$ left the queue first, so $d(y) \ge d(x)$.
- The part of $P$ from $y$ to $x$ has a cost greater than or equal to zero, because no edge
  has a negative weight.

Putting the three inequalities together:

$$
\text{cost}(P) \;\ge\; d(y) + \text{cost}(P_{y \to x}) \;\ge\; d(x)
$$

No path can therefore cost less than $d(x)$, and the value found is final.

:::caution[Negative weights]
The proof relies on the third point. With edges S→A of weight 2, S→B of weight 3 and B→A of
weight $-2$, A leaves first with cost 2 and becomes final; the path S→B→A, however, would
cost $3 - 2 = 1$. For graphs with negative weights the Bellman-Ford algorithm is used, which
does not make any node final before the end.
:::

## Rebuilding the path

At the end every node has a final label:

| Node | S | A | D | B | E | G | F | H | C | T |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cost from S | 0 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 17 |
| Via | — | S | S | A | B | S | E | F | B | H |

The path is read backwards starting from T, following the predecessors: T comes from H,
H from F, F from E, E from B, B from A, A from S. Read in the right direction, the path is
S → A → B → E → F → H → T, for a total of 17 minutes.

:::tip[Worth noticing]
The three "lane" paths cost 24 minutes (S-A-B-C-H-T), 20 minutes (S-D-E-F-H-T) and
23 minutes (S-G-F-H-T). The shortest one is a mixed path: it follows the upper lane as far
as B and switches to the middle lane through the 1-minute edge B→E. It is easy to miss at
a glance, while the algorithm finds it without any overall view of the network.
:::

## Adjacency matrix and adjacency list

For a program the graph is a data structure. In the **adjacency matrix** the row is the
starting node, the column is the arrival node, and the cell holds the weight of the edge, or
a dash if the edge does not exist:

| | S | A | B | C | D | E | F | G | H | T |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | – | 4 | – | – | 6 | – | – | 10 | – | – |
| **A** | – | – | 4 | – | – | – | – | – | – | – |
| **B** | – | – | – | 8 | – | 1 | – | – | – | – |
| **C** | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | 5 | – |
| **D** | – | – | – | – | – | 6 | 9 | – | – | – |
| **E** | – | – | – | – | – | – | 3 | – | – | – |
| **F** | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | 2 | – |
| **G** | – | – | – | – | – | – | 8 | – | – | – |
| **H** | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | 3 |
| **T** | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | – | – |

When a node becomes final, its outgoing edges are read on its row. The matrix, however, takes
up $n^2$ cells even when there are few edges: here 13 cells out of 100 are filled. This is
why the **adjacency list** is often preferred: for each node it keeps only the neighbours
with their weights, and it takes up memory proportional to the number of edges:

| Node | Neighbours (weight) |
|---|---|
| S | A (4), D (6), G (10) |
| A | B (4) |
| B | C (8), E (1) |
| C | H (5) |
| D | E (6), F (9) |
| E | F (3) |
| F | H (2) |
| G | F (8) |
| H | T (3) |
| T | none |

Matrix and list describe the graph; the priority queue, instead, holds the tentative costs
that change while the algorithm runs.

## Computational cost

With $n$ nodes and $m$ edges, the original version of the algorithm uses no queue: at every
step it scans all non-final nodes to find the one with the lowest cost, and it takes time
proportional to $n^2$. With a priority queue implemented as a **binary heap** (a binary tree
that always keeps the smallest element at the top) the time drops to
$O\big((m + n)\log n\big)$, a large gain on big networks with few edges per node. In 1984
Fredman and Tarjan proposed the Fibonacci heap, which brings the time down to
$O(m + n \log n)$.

## A bit of history

The algorithm was devised in 1956 by Edsger W. Dijkstra, then a programmer at the
Mathematisch Centrum in Amsterdam. He was looking for a problem that people unfamiliar with
computers could understand, in order to show what the new ARMAC computer could do, and he
chose the shortest route between two Dutch cities. In a 2001 interview he recalled working
it out in about twenty minutes, sitting on a café terrace during a morning of shopping with
his fiancée, without pencil or paper. He published it three years later, in 1959, in the
paper *A note on two problems in connexion with graphs* in the journal
*Numerische Mathematik*.

Today the algorithm, or one of its variants, underlies route planning in navigation systems
and network routing protocols such as OSPF (*Open Shortest Path First*), which decides which
routers the data travels through.
