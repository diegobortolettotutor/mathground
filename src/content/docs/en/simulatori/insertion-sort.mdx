Insertion Sort is the method often used to arrange playing cards in one's hand: one card at a time
is picked up and inserted in the right place among those already sorted. The list is split into
two parts: on the left the sorted part, which at the start contains only the first element, on the
right the part still to sort. In each pass the first element of the part still to sort, called the
**key**, is inserted into the sorted part.

The simulator accepts from 2 to 10 whole numbers between 0 and 99, separated by commas or spaces,
or it can generate random ones. When the simulator has the focus, the arrow keys move one step
forwards or backwards.

## The procedure

Given a list $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_{n-1}$ of $n$ elements, for $i$ from $1$ to $n - 1$:

1. the key $k = a_i$ is taken out of the list, leaving an empty cell at position $i$;
2. as long as the element to the left of the empty cell is greater than $k$, it is moved one cell
   to the right, and the empty cell moves one cell to the left;
3. the shifting stops when the element to the left is less than or equal to $k$, or when the empty
   cell has reached position 0, and $k$ is written into the empty cell.

A shift is not a swap: each element greater than the key is written only once, into the next cell,
and the key is written only once, at the end of the pass.

## How many comparisons and how many shifts

An **inversion** is a pair of elements, not necessarily neighbours, in which the larger one comes
before the smaller one. Each shift removes exactly one inversion, because the shifted element is
greater than the key and came before it. The shifts are therefore as many as the inversions of the
starting list: the same number as the swaps of [Bubble Sort](/en/simulatori/bubble-sort/), but each
one costs a single write instead of the three of a swap.

In each pass there is one comparison more than the shifts, except when the key goes all the way to
cell 0: there the comparison that stops the shifting is missing.

- Best case, list already sorted: every key stops at the first comparison, so $n - 1$ comparisons
  and no shifts are enough.
- Worst case, list in decreasing order: every key goes all the way to cell 0, and
  $\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$ comparisons and as many shifts are needed.

| Example | Inversions | Comparisons | Shifts |
|---|---|---|---|
| simple: 41, 73, 17, 56, 8, 92, 29 | 11 | 15 | 11 |
| advanced: 92, 85, 77, 23, 64, 41, 12 | 19 | 21 | 19 |

Insertion Sort is **adaptive**: the closer the list is to being sorted, the less work it does. For
this reason it is often preferred in practice for short or nearly sorted lists, even though in the
worst case the number of comparisons grows like $n^2$.

:::tip[Worth noticing]
When the key is greater than the whole sorted part, a single comparison is enough: in the simple
example this happens with 73 in pass 1 and with 92 in pass 5. With the list 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 it
happens in every pass, and the comparisons drop to 6.
:::

## Properties

- The algorithm works **in place**: it modifies the list itself and uses only one auxiliary
  variable for the key.
- The algorithm is **stable**: two equal elements keep their original relative order. The shifting
  stops in front of an element equal to the key, which is therefore not overtaken; the simulator
  points this out when it happens, for example with 5, 5, 3, 5, 1.

## A bit of history

Donald Knuth, in *The Art of Computer Programming*, reports that sorting by insertion was mentioned
by John Mauchly as early as 1946, in the first published discussion of computer sorting. Mauchly,
one of the designers of ENIAC, discussed it in the lecture *Sorting and Collating* of the summer
course held that year at the Moore School of the University of Pennsylvania, in Philadelphia.

Sources: D. E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming*, vol. 3, *Sorting and Searching*, p. 82;
[programme of the Moore School lectures, 1946](https://curation.cs.manchester.ac.uk/digital60/www.digital60.org/birth/themooreschool/list.html).
