Merge Sort applies the **divide and conquer** strategy: a large problem is broken into smaller
problems of the same kind, these are solved and the results are combined. The list is split in
half, each half is sorted with the same method, and the two sorted halves are **merged** into a
single sorted list. The splitting stops at lists of a single element, which are already sorted.

The upper part of the simulator shows the tree of the splits: each level is a level of recursion.
The lower part, during a merge, shows the two lists being merged and the result under construction.
The simulator accepts from 2 to 10 whole numbers between 0 and 99, separated by commas or spaces,
or it can generate random ones; when the simulator has the focus, the arrow keys move one step
forwards or backwards.

## The procedure

To sort a list of $n$ elements:

1. if $n = 1$ the list is already sorted and there is nothing to do;
2. otherwise it is split into two halves; with $n$ odd the first half has one more element;
3. the first half is sorted with the same procedure;
4. the second half is sorted with the same procedure;
5. the two sorted halves are merged.

The procedure is **recursive**: steps 3 and 4 call the whole procedure on a shorter list. This is
why in the simulator the left half is completed, up to its last merge, before the right half is
started.

## The merge

To merge two lists $L$ and $R$ that are already sorted, it is enough to compare their first
elements not yet copied: the smaller one is copied into the result and its list moves forward.
On a tie the left one is copied. When one of the two lists is finished, the elements left in the
other are copied with no further comparisons: they are already in order and are greater than or
equal to all those copied.

Every comparison produces a copy, and the last element is always copied with no comparison: a merge
with a result of $m$ elements therefore takes at most $m - 1$ comparisons. In the simple example the
last merge joins 17, 41, 56, 73 and 8, 29, 92 with 6 comparisons; once 73 has been copied the left
list is finished and 92 is copied with no comparison.

## How many comparisons

Every level of the tree contains all $n$ elements, so the merges of one level copy $n$ elements
with fewer than $n$ comparisons. Since the parts are halved at every level, there are
$\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ merge levels, and the total work is proportional to $n \log_2 n$, whatever
the starting order. More precisely, with $k = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ the maximum number of
comparisons is

$$
n k - 2^{k} + 1
$$

Both examples of the simulator reach this maximum: 14 comparisons with 7 elements and 25 with 10.
The difference from the elementary algorithms becomes huge on long lists: with 1000 elements
$n(n-1)/2$ is 499,500, while $n \log_2 n$ is about 10,000.

| Example | Splits | Copies | Comparisons |
|---|---|---|---|
| simple: 41, 73, 17, 56, 8, 92, 29 | 6 | 20 | 14 |
| advanced: 27, 90, 49, 83, 35, 58, 64, 71, 12, 96 | 9 | 34 | 25 |

The splits are always $n - 1$: each split increases the number of parts by one, from 1 to $n$. The
copies depend only on $n$: each element is copied once for every merge level it goes through.

:::tip[Worth noticing]
The comparisons of a merge depend on how the two lists interleave. If all the elements of one list
are smaller than all those of the other, the comparisons needed are as many as the elements of the
list that runs out first. With the list 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 the comparisons drop to 9, the minimum
possible with 7 elements.
:::

## Properties

- The algorithm does **not work in place**: the merge writes the result into an auxiliary list, so
  extra memory proportional to $n$ is needed.
- The algorithm is **stable**: two equal elements keep their original relative order, because on a
  tie the left one, which came first in the starting list, is copied first.

## A bit of history

Donald Knuth credits Merge Sort to John von Neumann, who in 1945 wrote it as a program for EDVAC,
the stored-program computer then being designed. It was one of the first programs in history,
written without being able to test it on a real machine. In 1970 Knuth analysed the original
manuscript in the paper *Von Neumann's First Computer Program*.
