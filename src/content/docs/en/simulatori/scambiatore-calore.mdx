Two identical fluids, one hot and one cold, flow through two adjacent channels that follow
the same coil. The channels are separated by a thin wall that lets heat through but not
the fluid: this is the principle behind every heat exchanger, from the plate exchangers in
boilers to the heat recovery units of home ventilation. At start-up the fluids are at rest
and at the same temperature; then the pumps set them in motion, and within about
3 seconds the inlet temperatures reach the chosen values.

## How to read the simulator

- The **fluid colour** shows its temperature, from blue (cold) to red (hot). The white
  dots move with the fluid and show its direction and speed.
- The **arrows** cross the wall and show the direction of heat flow, always from the
  hotter channel to the colder one. Their colour measures the temperature difference
  between the two channels: yellow where it is large, orange and then brown where it is
  small. Where the difference vanishes, the arrow disappears.
- The **plot** shows the temperature of both fluids as a function of position along the
  coil, measured from the hot fluid inlet.
- The **panel** at the bottom shows the time a parcel of fluid takes to travel through
  the coil, the temperature drop of the hot fluid, the effectiveness and the entropy
  produced, all described below.

## The model

Each channel is divided into 900 small fluid elements that move along the coil at the
chosen speed $v$. Two facing elements, at temperatures $T_h$ (hot) and $T_c$ (cold),
exchange in a short interval $\Delta t$ an amount of heat proportional to their
temperature difference. Since the fluids are identical and have the same flow rate, the
temperature one loses is the temperature the other gains:

$$
\Delta T_h = -k\,(T_h - T_c)\,\Delta t
\qquad\qquad
\Delta T_c = +k\,(T_h - T_c)\,\Delta t
$$

The constant $k = 0.25\ \text{s}^{-1}$ sums up the properties of the wall (material,
thickness, contact area) and the heat capacity of the fluid. It is the same law that describes a cup of tea cooling on
the table: the larger the temperature difference, the faster the exchange.

The coil is $L = 1.2\ \text{m}$ long, so a parcel of fluid stays in contact with the other
channel for the **transit time**

$$
\tau = \frac{L}{v}
$$

and it is this time, together with $k$, that decides how much heat can get through.

## Counterflow and parallel flow

The direction buttons let you compare the two possible configurations.

- In **parallel flow** both fluids enter from the same side. At the inlet the temperature
  difference is at its largest and the arrows are yellow; further along, the two
  temperatures approach each other and both tend to the average. The hot fluid cannot
  drop below the average, because at that point the exchange stops.
- In **counterflow** the fluids enter from opposite sides. As the hot fluid cools down, it
  meets colder and colder fluid: the temperature difference stays moderate and nearly
  uniform along the whole coil, and all the arrows have the same colour. At steady state
  the two profiles in the plot become parallel straight lines.

To compare them we use the **effectiveness** $\varepsilon$: the fraction of the largest
possible temperature drop that the hot fluid actually achieves.

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{T_{h,\text{in}} - T_{h,\text{out}}}{T_{h,\text{in}} - T_{c,\text{in}}}
$$

With $\varepsilon = 100\%$ the hot fluid would leave at the inlet temperature of the cold
fluid. The steady-state values depend on the speed:

| Speed   | Transit time | Counterflow | Parallel flow |
|:-------:|:------------:|:-----------:|:-------------:|
| 5 cm/s  | 24 s         | 86%         | 50%           |
| 10 cm/s | 12 s         | 75%         | 50%           |
| 20 cm/s | 6 s          | 60%         | 48%           |
| 40 cm/s | 3 s          | 43%         | 39%           |

Counterflow always wins, and the gap widens as the fluid slows down. In parallel flow the
effectiveness never exceeds 50%, however long the coil; in counterflow it can approach
100% if the fluid stays in contact long enough. One can show that, for two identical
fluids with the same flow rate, counterflow gives $\varepsilon = \dfrac{k\tau}{1 + k\tau}$.

:::tip[Try this]
Start the simulation in counterflow and wait for steady state, then switch to parallel
flow without stopping it: the yellow arrows gather at the inlet and the effectiveness
drops. Then try halving and doubling the speed and watch how the transit time and the
outlet temperature of the hot fluid change.
:::

## An application: heat recovery at home

In **mechanical ventilation** systems, in winter, warm stale indoor air is exhausted while
cold outdoor air comes in. Without a heat recovery unit, the heating system has to warm
all the fresh air from the outdoor to the indoor temperature. With a recovery unit, the
exhaust air gives heat to the incoming air, which enters already preheated: the
effectiveness $\varepsilon$ is exactly the fraction of heat recovered, while
$1 - \varepsilon$ is the fraction lost anyway.

For example, with 150 m³ of air per hour, 20 °C indoors and 0 °C outdoors, about 1000 W
is lost without recovery; with $\varepsilon = 75\%$ the air comes in at 15 °C and the loss
drops to about 250 W. This is why the most efficient recovery units are counterflow;
others are cross-flow, with effectiveness between the two simulated configurations.

## Entropy: where the waste is

When heat $Q$ flows from the hot fluid, at absolute temperature $T_h$, to the cold fluid,
at temperature $T_c$, Clausius' definition $\Delta S = Q/T$ gives:

- the hot fluid loses entropy $Q/T_h$;
- the cold fluid gains entropy $Q/T_c$.

Since $T_c < T_h$, the gain exceeds the loss and the total entropy increases:

$$
\Delta S = \frac{Q}{T_c} - \frac{Q}{T_h} > 0
$$

This is the second law at work: heat flows spontaneously only from hot to cold, and
every transfer produces entropy. How much, though, depends on the temperature difference.
For $Q = 100\ \text{J}$:

- from 80 °C to 10 °C, that is from 353 K to 283 K: $\Delta S \approx 0.070\ \text{J/K}$;
- from 50 °C to 40 °C, that is from 323 K to 313 K: $\Delta S \approx 0.010\ \text{J/K}$.

The same heat exchanged across a small temperature step produces about seven times less
entropy. **The waste lies in the temperature difference, not in the heat exchanged.**

The simulator applies this formula to every pair of facing elements, adds up over the
whole coil and divides by the total heat exchanged, showing the entropy produced per kJ.
With the initial settings, at steady state, it gives about 0.17 J/K in counterflow and
0.35 J/K in parallel flow: counterflow exchanges more heat and, per joule, produces about
half the entropy. The arrows show it at a glance, because their colour measures exactly
the temperature difference.

:::note[The model's idealisations]
The two fluids are identical and have the same flow rate; the constant $k$ is uniform
along the whole coil; heat does not travel along the channel, only across the wall; the
flow is orderly, with no turbulence, and friction and pressure losses are ignored. In a
real exchanger, longer and narrower channels improve the exchange but need more powerful
pumps: design looks for a compromise between the two effects.
:::
